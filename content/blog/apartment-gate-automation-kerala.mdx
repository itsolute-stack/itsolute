A gate serving one house and a gate serving thirty flats look identical and are not the same machine. The difference isn't the gate; it's everything around it — how often it moves, how many people may open it, who gets in when they have no credential, and who pays when it needs a service.

Specifying a shared entrance as though it were a large house gate is the most common and most expensive mistake in this category.

## Duty cycle is the whole specification

Start here, because everything else is cheaper to get wrong.

A house gate opens maybe six to ten times a day. A thirty-flat block, with residents, staff, deliveries and visitors, can easily run **three hundred to six hundred cycles a day**. Over a year that's the difference between a few thousand operations and roughly two hundred thousand.

Motors are rated for this, and the rating is a real engineering limit, not a marketing tier. A unit specified for intermittent residential use will fail on a shared entrance — not because anything was defective, but because it was the wrong machine for the job.

What makes this painful is the sequence: the cheaper quote wins the committee vote, the gate fails in eighteen months, and the association buys a second gate motor. The saving was never real.

So the first question for any shared entrance is not "what does it cost" but **"how many vehicles pass per day, and what cycle rating does that need?"** Count it honestly. Residents will underestimate.

## Gate, barrier, or both

These solve different problems:

| | Gate | Boom barrier |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purpose | Secures the boundary | Controls vehicle flow |
| Speed | Slower | A few seconds |
| Stops people on foot | Yes | No |
| High cycle counts | Needs heavy specification | Built for it |

For a complex with real traffic, the common answer is both: the **barrier** handling vehicles through the day, and the **gate** closed overnight for the perimeter. A barrier is a traffic device, not a security device — it stops cars, and anyone can walk around it.

If throughput at the entrance is the complaint — cars queuing on the road waiting for a slow gate — the barrier is what fixes it. [Boom barrier pricing and sizing](/blog/boom-barrier-price-kerala) covers arm length and the cycle classes.

## Access that can be revoked

On a shared entrance, access is an administrative problem wearing a hardware costume.

**Use RFID, per vehicle.** Not per flat — a flat with two cars needs two tags, or the tags get shared and the register becomes fiction.

**Keep a written register.** The controller stores tag numbers. A list of which number belongs to which flat and vehicle is what makes cancellation possible. Without it you have anonymous numbers and no way to act on a departure.

**Expect turnover.** Tenants change. That's the whole argument against shared remotes and shared keypad codes: when one person leaves, every other method requires re-issuing or re-coding for everybody, so in practice nobody does it, and the gate's access list grows permanently. [RFID versus remotes](/blog/rfid-vs-remote-gate-access) goes through the trade-off properly.

A shared keypad code in a thirty-flat block is a code the neighbourhood knows within a year. This is not a hypothetical.

## The visitor question

Every complex needs a defined answer to: *a delivery rider arrives with no credential.*

Pick one before installation:

- **Guard button** — simplest, needs someone present.
- **Intercom to the flats** — the resident decides, no guard needed.
- **Keypad code for visitors** — convenient, and only honest if someone actually changes it periodically.

If you don't choose, the answer becomes "the guard's phone rings all day", and after a few weeks someone props the gate open. A propped-open gate is the real failure mode of shared entrance automation, and it's always an access-design problem rather than a hardware one.

## Safety on a shared entrance

Shared entrances have genuine foot traffic — residents walking out, children, delivery riders — crossing the same opening the vehicles use. That raises the safety bar above a private driveway.

Two things to specify rather than assume:

- **More than one photocell pair, or a safety edge.** One beam across one line is a single plane. On a busy opening with people moving through it, that's thin coverage.
- **A separate pedestrian gate.** The single biggest safety improvement available, and the most often skipped. People should not be walking through a vehicle gate at all.

[Gate safety](/blog/automatic-gate-safety-sensors) covers what the sensors do and don't protect.

## Power cuts matter more here

A house gate that stops in an outage is an inconvenience for one family. A shared entrance that stops blocks everybody, including anyone trying to leave.

Battery backup is fitted as standard on our installations, and on a shared entrance two extra points apply:

- **The manual release must be known to the security staff**, not just to a committee member — and the key must not live behind the gate it opens.
- **The backup battery is a wear part**, and on a high-cycle gate it works harder. It needs load-testing on a schedule. See [gates and power cuts](/blog/automatic-gate-power-cut-battery-backup).

Emergency vehicle access is the version of this nobody wants to discover late. An ambulance at a dead gate at 2am is the scenario the manual release exists for.

## Decide who owns the maintenance

The organisational point, and the one that decides whether the installation is still working in five years.

A shared gate wears several times faster than a residential one, so **quarterly** maintenance is realistic rather than cautious. But a gate that belongs to "the association" in the abstract belongs to nobody, and gets serviced when it breaks.

Settle three things in writing at handover:

1. **Who holds the maintenance contract** and what it covers.
2. **Who keeps the RFID register** and processes joiners and leavers.
3. **Who holds the manual release key**, and their backup.

[Gate maintenance in Kerala](/blog/automatic-gate-maintenance-amc-kerala) sets out what a service visit actually checks and why the battery and sensors are the items that fail without warning.

## What we'd put in a quote for a complex

- A motor rated for the measured daily cycle count, not the gate's weight alone
- **Safety sensors, battery backup, and remote plus RFID access** as standard
- Additional photocells or a safety edge where foot traffic justifies it
- A defined visitor method, chosen by the association, not defaulted
- A barrier alongside the gate where vehicle throughput is the real constraint
- A quarterly maintenance schedule with a named owner
- A free site survey first, including an honest cycle count

## The bottom line

Specify a shared entrance for the traffic it actually carries, use access credentials you can cancel one at a time, decide the visitor route before installation rather than after, and name someone to own the maintenance. Get those four right and the hardware is the easy part.
