In Kerala, "what happens when the power goes?" isn't a hypothetical. It's the question that should decide whether you accept a gate automation quote.

The answer is straightforward, but it has a few parts worth understanding before you buy — particularly because this is the component most often quietly removed from a cheaper quote.

## Three ways a gate behaves in an outage

**With battery backup fitted:** nothing happens. The control board switches to the battery and the gate opens and closes as normal. For a typical outage, you won't notice.

**Without backup:** the gate stops. It stays wherever it was — which, if the power went mid-cycle, may be half open. You then use the manual release to move it by hand.

**With backup, but a dead battery:** the same as having no backup. This is the case people don't plan for, and it's the most common one we're called about, because a fading battery gives no warning.

## How the backup works

The battery sits with the control board at the motor. In normal operation mains power runs the gate and trickle-charges the battery. When the mains drops, the board switches over, and the gate runs on stored charge.

Two consequences worth knowing:

1. **It is independent of your house supply.** The backup is local to the gate, on its own wiring. It doesn't care whether your inverter is working or how your house circuits are arranged.

2. **It is always either charging or discharging.** A battery kept in a hot enclosure and cycled regularly ages faster than the datasheet suggests. In Kerala's heat, assume the shorter end of any stated life.

## How many openings do you really get?

Be sceptical of anyone who answers this with a confident single number. It depends on:

- **Gate weight** — a heavy gate draws more current per cycle, so fewer cycles.
- **Battery capacity** — larger battery, more cycles.
- **Battery age and condition** — the dominant factor after a couple of years.
- **How long the outage runs** — a long outage also drains the board's standby draw.

A healthy backup on a typical residential gate covers a normal day's use without drama. The useful way to think about it isn't the day-one number — it's that the number declines, and you won't be told when.

## The manual release

Every automated gate has a way to disengage the motor so the gate can be moved by hand. Usually a key or a lever at the motor.

Three things to insist on:

- **Be shown it at handover**, physically, and try it yourself once.
- **Know where the key lives**, and make sure it isn't only inside the gate you're locked out of. This sounds obvious. It is the single most common lockout we get called about.
- **Make sure household staff or security know it too**, not just you.

For a boom barrier the equivalent matters even more, because emergency vehicles may need the arm raised immediately.

## What to ask a quote for

Battery backup is fitted as standard on every entrance automation job we do, along with safety sensors and remote plus RFID access. If you're comparing quotes, these are exactly the lines to check:

- **Is battery backup included?** If the quote doesn't say, assume not.
- **Is it a dedicated gate battery** at the motor, or is the quote assuming your inverter will cover it?
- **Is the battery a replaceable, standard part**, so it can be swapped in a few years without replacing the control board?
- **Who checks it, and when?**

A quote that's noticeably cheaper has usually removed the backup, the photocells, or both. That isn't a discount on the same system — it's a different system. Our [automatic gate price breakdown](/blog/automatic-gate-price-kerala) covers the rest of the comparison.

## Do you need an inverter as well?

Generally no, and running the gate from the house inverter instead of a dedicated backup is a step down in reliability: you've made the gate dependent on another system, its wiring, and its own battery's health.

If you already have an inverter, feeding the gate from it is a reasonable belt-and-braces addition. It's not a replacement for the gate's own backup.

## The maintenance point

The backup battery is the one part of a gate installation guaranteed to need replacing. Everything else — motor, board, sensors — should run for years with adjustment. The battery is a consumable, and its decline is invisible until the day it matters.

That's the practical argument for an AMC on an automated gate: the visit checks battery health, sensor alignment, force limits, and the track or hinges, which together are almost everything that causes a gate to fail. Nothing on the gate will tell you the battery is fading, so either someone checks it on a schedule or you find out during an outage.

## The bottom line

A properly specified gate rides out a power cut without you noticing, keeps a manual release for when it can't, and has its battery checked before it fails rather than after.
