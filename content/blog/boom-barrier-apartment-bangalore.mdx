Specifying a barrier for an apartment complex is a different exercise from buying one for a house. The equipment is the easy part. The hard parts are getting a decision made, choosing an access method you can administer for years, and agreeing who owns the thing afterwards.

This is a guide for whoever has been handed that job.

## Start with the traffic, not the lane

The single most common specification error is sizing a barrier by the width of the entrance.

Lane width tells you the arm length. **Duty cycle tells you which barrier you need.** A 300-flat complex can cycle a barrier a thousand times a day, most of it concentrated into a morning window and an evening one. A barrier specified like a small office installation will fail inside a year, and it will look like a dud product rather than a specification mistake.

Before anyone quotes, get a rough count: how many vehicles live here, how many move on a weekday, and when. Any supplier who does not ask for that is giving you a price rather than a specification.

## The approval problem

Most of the time, installation is not the slow part. Approval is.

What makes a committee decision slow is usually a quote that cannot be read — one number, no breakdown, nothing to compare. What makes it quick is a document that answers the questions members will ask before they ask them:

- **What does the barrier itself cost?**
- **What does installation cost, and what is civil work?**
- **What does the access method cost for our actual number of flats?**
- **What will it cost us every year?**

That last one is the question that derails second meetings. Put the expected service interval and the AMC cost in the first document and the conversation moves on.

We write quotes in that form, and we are willing to answer technical questions from the association directly rather than having you relay them. We also hold the price through the approval period rather than re-quoting after a month, because re-pricing an approved proposal is how suppliers lose associations.

## Access: the decision you live with

Two remotes come with the barrier. For a complex, that is where the real decision starts.

**Remotes** work when the building is small and the resident list is stable. Everyone knows whose car is whose.

**RFID tags or cards**, issued per vehicle, work at scale — and the argument for them is administrative rather than technical. A flat changes hands every so often. With tags, you deactivate the old one. With remotes, you ask for it back and sometimes receive it.

Over five years, in a building of any size, that difference is the whole ballgame. The complexes that regret their choice are nearly always the ones that chose remotes for a hundred-flat building because it was cheaper on day one.

RFID is an add-on quoted after the survey, since the cost scales with credential count and read points.

## The guard workflow

Whatever you install has to be operable by whoever is on duty at two in the morning, without a manual and without calling anyone.

That sounds obvious and is routinely ignored. Three things worth settling before installation:

1. **What does the guard do for an expected visitor?** If the answer involves phoning each flat, your bottleneck is the visitor process, not the barrier.
2. **What does the guard do when a resident has forgotten their tag?** There should be an answer that is not "leave the barrier up".
3. **What does the guard do when it fails?** There is a manual release. Everyone on the rota should have been shown it.

A video door phone at the entrance addresses the first of those directly — the panel calls the flat, the resident sees the visitor, and the guard is out of the loop. For a busy gate it is often the better investment of the two.

## Safety on a shared entrance

A photocell pair across the lane is included in every barrier we quote. On a shared entrance it earns its place faster than anywhere else, because the traffic includes residents' children, delivery riders and people walking through a lane designed for cars.

If a quote you are comparing omits photocells, it is not a cheaper version of the same barrier. It is a different proposition, and the committee should be told that explicitly.

## Power cuts

A barrier stuck down blocks everyone. A barrier stuck up is no barrier at all.

Battery backup is two 12V batteries — built in on some brands, a ₹5,500 add-on on others. For a shared entrance we treat it as part of the specification rather than an option, and we would say so even if you had not asked.

## Who owns it afterwards

The question associations most often fail to settle. A barrier is not a one-time purchase; it is a piece of machinery that cycles more than anything else on the property.

Arm balance drifts. Spring tension changes. Loop detectors need testing. Tags accumulate with people who have moved out. All of that is predictable, which is exactly why a scheduled AMC is a better deal for a complex than call-outs after failures — the failures that strand a building are usually the ones a visit would have caught.

Decide at the outset whether the AMC sits with the association or the facilities contractor, and put it in the same document as the quote.

## The order to do things in

1. Count the traffic.
2. Get a quote that separates equipment, access and installation, with the annual cost named.
3. Decide remotes vs tags on a five-year view, not a day-one price.
4. Agree the visitor process before installation, not after.
5. Agree who owns maintenance.
6. Then approve it.
