An industrial entrance is not a large version of a residential one. It fails differently, it is bought by someone asking different questions, and specifying it like a house gate is how a motor ends up replaced inside a year.

This is what changes.

## Duty cycle is the specification

The number that matters is **how often it moves**.

A house gate opens perhaps eight times a day. A factory yard gate across three shifts, with goods vehicles and staff traffic, can move two hundred times — and a barrier at a commercial car park can exceed that comfortably.

What makes this harder than it sounds is that the load is rarely even. Most of a site's cycles happen in short windows at shift change, which is more punishing than the daily total suggests.

So the first questions we ask at an industrial survey are not about the opening:

- How many vehicles pass on a typical day?
- When do they pass?
- What is the heaviest thing that goes through?
- Do people walk through the same opening?

A quote produced without those answers is a price, not a specification. If you are comparing quotes and only one supplier asked, that difference tells you something.

## Heavy gates, measured

Industrial gates are built for security. Heavier section, solid infill, sometimes sheeting — all of which means a gate the same width as a residential one can weigh substantially more.

Sizing a motor from the opening width is therefore wrong in exactly the situation where being wrong is most expensive. We measure the gate and assess how it is built.

Occasionally the finding is that the gate itself needs work before a motor is worth fitting — a frame that flexes when driven from one end, or a track that has settled under years of heavy use. That goes in the quote as a separate line rather than being discovered mid-installation.

## Gate or barrier

They solve different problems and plenty of sites need both.

**A boom barrier** controls vehicles quickly. It does not secure an opening — an arm is not a wall. It suits a lane where throughput matters: car parks, controlled yard access during working hours, anywhere a queue would form.

**A sliding gate** closes an opening properly. It is slower, and on a busy lane that slowness is the whole problem.

The common pattern at a Karnataka industrial site is a barrier on the working vehicle lane and a gate that closes the compound outside hours. Planning both together is cheaper than doing them a year apart, because the cabling and the civil work overlap.

## Safety where vehicles and people share an opening

At many smaller industrial sites there is no separate pedestrian route. People walk through the vehicle gate because it is the only gate.

A photocell pair is included in everything we quote. But where the layout itself puts walkers in a vehicle lane, we will say that the layout needs addressing rather than treating sensors as a complete answer. Sensors stop a gate closing on someone; they do not make a shared lane a good idea.

## What downtime costs

This is the argument for scheduled servicing, and at an industrial site it is a real calculation rather than a sales line.

A gate stuck at a house inconveniences one family. A barrier stuck at a works entrance at shift change holds vehicles on a public road and delays everyone arriving or leaving.

The components that fail at an industrial entrance — arm balance, spring tension, loop detectors, a motor under sustained load — fail **predictably**. That predictability is exactly what a scheduled interval exists to catch, which is why an AMC is a better proposition here than call-outs after failures.
