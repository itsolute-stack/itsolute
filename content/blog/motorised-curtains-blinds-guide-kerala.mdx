Motorised curtains are one of those things that sound indulgent until you live with a window you cannot comfortably reach. A tall stairwell window, a wide living room run, a bedroom where the first act of every morning is crossing the room — those are the cases where a motor stops being a gadget.

Here is what decides whether it works at your windows, and what it costs you in disruption.

## Two kinds of motor

Which one you need is decided by what hangs at the window, not by preference.

**Track motors** drive a curtain along its track. The motor sits at one end and pulls the curtain across, which is why a wide or heavy pair opens evenly rather than bunching. This is what goes on conventional curtains, and it is the common case in living rooms and bedrooms.

**Tubular motors** sit inside the roller tube of a blind. Nothing is visible at the window — the tube simply turns. This is the standard approach for roller blinds and similar.

The two are not interchangeable, and a window with both curtains and a blind needs both.

## Can your existing curtains be motorised?

Usually. The curtains are rarely the obstacle.

**The track is.** What matters is its type, its condition, and whether it can carry the weight smoothly. Here is the test you can do yourself before anyone visits: draw the curtain by hand, slowly, the whole way across.

- If it runs smoothly end to end, the track is probably a candidate.
- If it catches, drags, or needs a tug at a particular point, a motor will not fix that. It will fight it, every single day, and lose sooner than it should.

A sticking track is not a reason to abandon the idea — it is a reason to replace the track as part of the job. What it is not is something to motorise and hope about. Where we find one, we say so in the quote.

## Wiring, and how disruptive it is

The motor needs power at the window. How much that costs you depends almost entirely on timing.

**During construction or renovation**, it is nothing. A point near the track is planned with the rest of the electrical work and you never see it.

**In a finished room**, it depends on what is nearby. Sometimes there is an existing point close enough. Sometimes it is a short run that can follow a cornice or skirting. Occasionally it is genuinely awkward, and that is worth knowing before you commit rather than on the day.

There is a third option: some motors are rechargeable, avoiding a permanent point altogether. The trade is that you charge them periodically. For one bedroom window that is a reasonable deal; for eight windows across a house it becomes a chore, and we would steer you to wiring instead.

This is the single biggest reason the survey matters more than the model choice.

## Noise

The honest answer: quieter than people expect, not silent.

A correctly sized motor on a clean track makes a low hum for the few seconds it runs. In a bedroom at 6am you will hear it, and most people find it preferable to crossing the room.

What is worth understanding is that **noise is usually a symptom**. A motor that sounds strained is either undersized for the weight it is pulling or running on a track that needs cleaning. Both shorten its life. So the quiet installation and the durable installation are not two different things — sizing the motor properly to the actual weight gets you both.

## Heavy curtains

Heavy lined curtains across a wide run are the strongest case for motorising, because they are the hardest to pull by hand. They are also where an underpowered motor fails fastest.

The thing to know is that weight is not predictable from width. Two curtains of identical width can differ substantially once lining, interlining and fabric weight are accounted for. That is why we judge the weight at the window rather than estimating it from a measurement, and why "what size is the window" is not sufficient information to quote from.

If your curtains are heavy, say so early. It does not make the job harder — it makes specifying it correctly more important.

## Power cuts

The motor stops. That part is simple.

What varies is whether you can still work the curtain by hand. **On some models the motor disengages** and the curtain draws manually with a firm pull. **On others it holds position** and will not move until power returns.

Neither is wrong, but they are very different to live with, and it is worth settling before purchase rather than discovering it during an outage. Tell us if manual operation matters and we will specify accordingly. Where a room genuinely cannot be left without it, the motors can go on your inverter connection, the same as other automation in the house.

## Remote, app, voice and schedules

**Remote control is standard** on everything we fit. One remote usually handles several motors, so a room of windows moves together rather than one at a time. For most households, this is the feature that gets used daily and the rest is extra.

**On selected models** you can add:

- **App control**, for operating from a phone.
- **Alexa or Google Home**, on compatible devices.
- **Schedules**, opening in the morning and closing at dusk without anyone touching anything.

Schedules are the one people underestimate. A house that opens its own curtains at 7am and closes them at dusk looks lived-in whether or not anyone is home, which is worth something if the property is sometimes empty.

As with everything model-dependent, these cannot be added later to a motor that does not support them. If you want them, they belong in the specification.

## Rooms worth doing first

If you are starting with one or two windows rather than a whole house, the ones that repay it most are:

- **The tall or awkward window** nobody can comfortably reach.
- **The widest run** in the living room, where pulling by hand is a two-handed job.
- **The bedroom**, for the schedule rather than the remote.

Offices and meeting rooms are a different argument — there it is about managing glare during a presentation without someone standing up mid-sentence.
