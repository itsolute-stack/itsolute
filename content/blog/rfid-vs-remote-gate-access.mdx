Access is the part of gate automation people think least about before buying and complain about most afterwards. The hardware works; the problem is almost always administrative — someone left, a remote went missing, the watchman can't let a visitor in.

So choose the access method by asking one question: **how often does the list of people who may open this gate change?**

## The four options

**Remotes.** A handheld transmitter. Press, gate opens. Cheap, instant, works from inside a vehicle in the rain. Anonymous — the gate knows a valid signal arrived, not who sent it.

**RFID cards or tags.** A credential held near a reader, or a windscreen tag read as the vehicle approaches. Issued individually, cancelled individually, and the controller knows which tag was used.

**Keypad.** A code. No credential to carry or lose, which is its advantage and its weakness — codes get shared, and changing one means telling everybody.

**Intercom or guard button.** A person decides. Slow, and needs someone present, but it's the only method that handles a visitor nobody anticipated.

Remote and RFID are included as standard on our installations. Keypads and intercom integration are quoted on top.

## The lost-credential test

This is the question that actually decides it, and it's worth being blunt about.

| Method | A credential is lost or someone leaves |
| --- | --- |
| RFID | Cancel that one tag. Nobody else is affected. |
| Remote | The remote still works. You either accept that, or re-pair the gate and re-issue to everyone. |
| Keypad | The code is still valid. Change it and tell everybody the new one. |

For a household of four with two cars, that risk is small and remotes are fine. For an apartment block with tenants moving in and out, or a business with staff turnover, it isn't — and the inconvenience compounds every time it happens.

This is why RFID is the default on anything shared. Not because it's more secure in a cryptographic sense, but because it's **revocable per person**.

## By site type

**A home, fixed household.** Remotes, with RFID as the fallback. Add a keypad only if you regularly need to let yourself in without a remote. Simple beats clever.

**A home with staff or tenants.** RFID, one tag each. You'll be glad of it the first time someone leaves.

**An apartment or villa complex.** RFID for residents — ideally one tag per vehicle, not per flat, so the list reflects reality — plus a guard button or intercom for visitors. A shared code on a keypad in a block of thirty flats is a code the whole neighbourhood knows within a year. More on the shared-entrance specifics in [apartment gate automation](/blog/apartment-gate-automation-kerala).

**A commercial entrance or parking.** RFID with a maintained tag list, plus a visitor method. Where vehicles queue, a windscreen tag read at a distance beats a card someone has to wind the window down for. This is also where [boom barriers](/blog/boom-barrier-price-kerala) usually make more sense than a gate.

## Where access and safety meet

One thing worth saying plainly: none of these methods is a safety device.

A credential controls *who* may open the gate. It does nothing about *what is in the way* when the gate moves. That's the job of the photocells and the force limit, which is a separate question and the more important one — see [automatic gate safety](/blog/automatic-gate-safety-sensors).

The two interact in one respect, though. Automatic access methods — a windscreen tag read as the vehicle rolls up, or an app triggered from down the road — mean the gate starts moving when nobody is necessarily watching it. The more hands-off the access, the more the safety sensors are doing the thinking, and the less acceptable it is to have only the bare minimum fitted.

## Practical details people wish they'd known

- **Tag per vehicle, not per household.** Two cars in a flat means two tags. Sharing one tag between vehicles defeats the point and causes arguments.
- **Keep a written tag register.** The controller holds the tags; a list of which tag belongs to whom is what makes cancelling possible. Without it you have a list of anonymous numbers.
- **Decide the visitor route before handover.** Every site needs an answer to "a delivery arrives and has no credential". If you don't choose one, the answer defaults to someone's phone ringing.
- **Reader position matters.** A reader mounted where a car's driver can't reach it means winding down, leaning out, or getting out. Mount it on the driver's side at window height, set back far enough that the car isn't blocking the road while it reads.
- **Remotes are consumables.** Batteries die and cases break. Keep a spare, and know it can be re-paired.

## What about voice control?

It comes up, so: voice control is a **WiFi smart-switch module** added to the control side. It works with most standard gate motors that have a remote-control input, and it's brand-agnostic.

Treat it as a convenience layer on top of a proper credential, not as an access method in itself. It depends on your WiFi and on a cloud service being up, and it offers no way to revoke access for one person. Useful, not foundational.

## The bottom line

If the list of people who may open your gate never changes, remotes are enough. The moment it does change — tenants, staff, drivers, a block of flats — RFID earns its place, because you can cancel one tag without disturbing anyone else. Add one visitor method, keep a tag register, and don't mistake any of it for a safety feature.
