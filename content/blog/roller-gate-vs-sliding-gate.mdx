"Roller gate" is one of the most confusing terms in this trade, because two completely different products answer to it.

Clearing that up first makes the rest of the comparison useful.

## What a roller gate is not

It is **not a rolling shutter**. A rolling shutter is a slatted curtain that rolls up into a box above the opening — the thing on a shopfront or a godown. We do not install those, and if that is what you need, the sooner you know we are the wrong supplier the better.

## What a roller gate is

A **swing gate carried on motorised wheels.**

The leaf is hinged at the pillar like any swing gate. Instead of an arm pushing it from the pillar, a motorised wheel at the bottom of the leaf drives along the ground and carries the gate round.

So it swings like a swing gate, but it is driven from below rather than from the side.

## How it compares with a sliding gate

These are the two that get compared most, and they fail in opposite situations.

| | Sliding gate | Roller (wheel-drive) gate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How it opens | Rolls sideways along a track | Swings on hinges, carried by a wheel |
| Space needed | A run alongside the opening to park into | The full arc clear inside the driveway |
| Handles slope | Poorly — the track must be level | Well — the wheel follows the ground |
| Needs good ground | Along the track line | Across the arc the wheel travels |
| From (incl. GST) | ₹39,000 (AC) / ₹44,500 (DC) | ₹51,000 (DC) |

**The deciding question is which kind of space you have.**

A sliding gate needs roughly the width of the opening again, alongside it. If a boundary wall, a tree or a parking bay interrupts that run, sliding is not available at any price.

A roller gate needs the arc inside the driveway clear instead. If your driveway is short and the car lives in it, that is the constraint you cannot meet.

Plenty of sites can satisfy one and not the other. That is normally what settles it.

## Where the roller gate genuinely wins

### Sloped approaches

This is the strongest case, and the one people most often arrive at the hard way.

An arm motor assumes the leaf travels through a reasonably level arc. Where the ground rises or falls through that arc, the arm fights the change and binds — the gate works for a while, then starts stalling at the same point every time. The usual conclusion is "the motor is underpowered", and it is wrong. The motor is fine; the geometry is not.

A wheel motor carries the leaf from below and follows whatever the ground does. On the sloped plots common toward the north and east of Bengaluru, this is the difference between a gate that works in year three and one that does not.

### Uneven or settled ground

Same reasoning. An approach that has settled unevenly over the years is hard on an arm and manageable for a wheel.

### Heavy leaves

Carrying a leaf from underneath is mechanically kinder than pushing it from one end. On a wide, heavy gate that matters.

## Where it is the wrong answer

**If you have side space and level ground**, a sliding gate is simpler and cheaper — ₹39,000 against ₹51,000 — and simpler is worth something.

**If the arc is blocked**, a roller gate does not help. It still swings. A short driveway with a parked car rules out roller and arm-type both, and the answer is sliding.

**If the running surface is poor**, it needs fixing first. The wheel rolls on your driveway, so loose gravel, a cracked section or a settled patch under the arc all mean the motor is working against the ground rather than the gate.

## The honest ranking

For most sites, in order of what we would try first:

1. **Sliding** — if there is a run alongside and the ground is level. Cheapest, simplest, fewest things to go wrong.
2. **Arm-type swing** — if the arc is clear and the approach is level.
3. **Wheel-drive roller** — where neither of those is true.

A roller gate is a solution to a specific problem rather than an upgrade. If a supplier is steering you to one without having measured the slope across your gate's arc or looked at your side space, ask why.
