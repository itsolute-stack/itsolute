A swing gate has either one leaf or two. Which one you have is usually already decided — it is the gate standing in front of you. What matters is understanding why the distinction changes the automation, and when a gate should be changed rather than automated as it is.

## The basic rule

**One arm motor per leaf.** A single-leaf gate takes one. A double-leaf gate takes two.

There is no arrangement where one motor operates two leaves, because the leaves move independently on their own hinges. What makes a double gate behave like one gate is **synchronisation**: the two motors set to start together, travel at matched speed and finish together.

## Why synchronisation is the real work

Two motors that are not properly matched produce a gate that is obviously wrong. One leaf arrives ahead of the other. They meet badly at the centre. On closing, the leading leaf reaches its stop and waits while the second catches up.

Beyond looking poor, this wears unevenly: the leaf that arrives first takes the impact, every cycle.

Setting this up is part of commissioning rather than an extra, and it is why a double gate takes longer to install than a single one. If someone quotes a double gate at exactly twice the single price with the same timeline, they have priced two motors and not the work of making them behave as one.

## Leverage: why wide single leaves are a bad idea

This is the part worth understanding if you are specifying a new gate rather than automating an existing one.

A swing leaf is a lever. The hinge is the pivot, and the weight of the gate acts along its length. Double the width and you have more than doubled what the hinge is being asked to carry, because both the weight and the distance have increased.

A wide single leaf therefore:

- loads one hinge set heavily
- sags over time, which changes the arc the motor drives
- needs a larger motor to overcome that leverage
- sweeps a bigger arc through your driveway

Splitting the same opening into two leaves halves each lever. The gate is kinder to its hinges, each leaf sweeps less, and the motors work within themselves.

**If you are having a gate fabricated for a wide opening, two leaves is the better specification**, and it is worth saying so to the fabricator before anything is welded.

## The arc problem

Both single and double swing gates have the same constraint: **the full arc has to stay clear.**

The practical test, before anyone quotes:

1. Park where you normally park.
2. Open the gate by hand, all the way.
3. Watch where the leaf goes.

If it comes near the car, a wall, a plant pot or a step, automating it does not help — it simply makes the collision automatic.

A double gate is more forgiving here, because each leaf sweeps a shorter arc. That is often the deciding factor on a compact plot, and it is a better reason to choose a double than the aesthetics.

## What the motor needs from the gate

Whether single or double, three things decide whether an arm motor will work well:

**Hinge condition.** Worn or dropped hinges change the arc. The motor absorbs that difference on every cycle until something gives.

**Pillar soundness.** An arm motor transfers real force into the pillar. One that is adequate for hanging a gate is not automatically adequate for driving it.

**Frame stiffness.** The arm pushes at one point. A leaf that flexes under that push transmits the drive badly.

All three are checked at the survey, and any of them can mean preparation before automation.

## When swing is the wrong family entirely

If the arc cannot be kept clear, the answer is not a better swing motor.

**Sliding** needs no clearance inside the driveway — only a run alongside the opening to park into. On a short driveway with side space, it is almost always the right call.

**Wheel-drive roller** gates are swing gates carried by motorised wheels at the base, which handles sloped or uneven approaches where an arm would bind. They still need the arc clear, so they solve a ground problem rather than a space one.

## Summary

- One motor per leaf, always.
- A double costs more and takes longer because synchronisation is real work.
- Beyond a certain width, two leaves is the better gate regardless of motor cost.
- The arc has to be clear either way — test it with the car where it actually sits.
