Most people do not replace a working lock because they want an app. They replace it because too many keys exist, or because somebody is always locked out, or because the spare lives with a neighbour who is not always in.

A smart lock solves that specific problem well. Here is what to settle before buying one.

## The ways in

Smart locks offer some combination of these. **Which combination depends entirely on the model**, so treat the list as a menu rather than a specification:

- **Fingerprint.** The fastest for the people who live there. Register once, open with a touch. Readers can struggle with wet or very dry hands, which in practice means the occasional second attempt rather than a lockout.
- **PIN code.** The most flexible. No enrolment, nothing to carry, and you can change it in seconds if you think someone has seen it.
- **RFID card or tag.** Useful where somebody would rather not register a fingerprint, or for an older relative who finds a tap easier than a keypad.
- **Mobile app.** Unlocking over Bluetooth or Wi-Fi, available on selected models. Also where access logs and remotely-issued codes live, where the model supports them.
- **Mechanical key.** The override. Every sensible lock keeps one.

The honest framing is that the first two cover ninety per cent of daily use, and the rest are conveniences worth paying for only if you will genuinely use them.

## The battery question, answered properly

This is the first thing everyone asks and the least alarming once explained.

Smart locks are battery powered, and they are insistent about it. A lock approaching the end of its batteries beeps or flashes at every single unlock, for days. Running one completely flat is not something that sneaks up on you — it takes ignoring a daily, deliberately annoying reminder.

If it does happen, the mechanical key opens the door. Which leads to the one rule worth repeating: **keep that key somewhere other than inside the house it opens.** A key in the drawer behind the locked door is not a backup. Some models also accept an emergency power source at the keypad to get you in long enough to swap batteries, but that varies by model and is not a substitute for the key.

Changing the batteries is a two-minute job we show you at handover.

## Whether it fits your door

This is the question that actually rules models in or out, and it has nothing to do with features.

What matters is the **door material**, its **thickness**, the **frame**, and the **existing lock cutout**. Wooden and metal doors are both perfectly workable. Problems appear at the extremes: a door thinner than a model's minimum, thicker than its maximum, or already cut for an unusual lock that leaves nowhere sensible to mount the new one.

None of that is visible from a photograph, which is why we measure at the survey. Discovering a thickness problem with a door half drilled is the bad version of this job, and it is entirely avoidable.

## Humidity, monsoon and the thing that actually fails

Smart locks on a sheltered front door hold up well in Kerala. The failure people describe as "the lock started playing up in the monsoon" is usually not the lock.

It is the door. Timber swells in sustained damp. A door that closed cleanly in February can sit a few millimetres proud in July, and a bolt that no longer meets its strike plate squarely strains every time it throws. The motor works harder, the batteries drain faster, and eventually something gives.

The fix is fitting rather than electronics: set the alignment with that seasonal movement in mind, and re-check it. It is the single most useful thing an AMC visit does on a smart lock, and the reason we treat alignment as part of the installation rather than a detail at the end.

For an exposed door with no porch, say so at the survey — it changes what we recommend.

## Temporary access, which is usually the real reason

Ask someone why they want a smart lock and the answer is rarely "fingerprints". It is that four people have keys and one of them has moved away.

On selected models you can issue a code to a guest, to domestic help, or for a delivery, and remove it afterwards. On app-connected models you can set one that expires on its own. The difference from a key is the part that matters: a lent key depends on someone returning it, while an issued code depends on nothing.

This is worth being explicit about when choosing, because it is a model-dependent feature that people assume is universal. If withdrawable access is the reason you are buying, it needs to be in the specification, not hoped for.

## Pairing it with the rest of the entrance

A lock on its own is useful. A lock that works with what is already at your gate is better.

On compatible setups, a [video door phone](/entrance-automation/video-door-phones) can release the lock from the indoor monitor — you see who is there, you speak to them, you let them in, without walking to the door. The same indoor unit can trigger an [automatic gate](/entrance-automation/automatic-gates), so the gate and the front door answer to the same press.

Whether a particular lock and a particular door phone will talk to each other depends on both. We check the specific combination before quoting, because "these should work together" is not something worth finding out after purchase.

## Is it actually secure?

Worth addressing directly, because it is the quiet worry under the convenience.

The lock still throws a physical bolt into the frame. That part is no different from a conventional lock, and it is what actually resists force. What changes is authorisation: fingerprints and codes cannot be copied the way a key can, and a compromised code is changed in seconds rather than requiring a locksmith.

The weak point, when there is one, is fitting. A smart lock on a door that does not close squarely is weaker than a simple lock on a door that does — which brings it back to alignment, again.
