A video door phone answers one question before you open anything: who is actually out there. For a house set back from its gate, that is the difference between walking out in the rain to find a courier and glancing at a screen.

The decisions that matter are smaller than the brochures suggest. Here are the ones that actually change what gets installed.

## The two parts

Every system is an outdoor unit and an indoor monitor.

The **outdoor unit** sits at the gate or the door. It holds a camera, a microphone and speaker, and a call button. It lives outdoors, which matters more in Kerala than the spec sheet admits — it will take monsoon rain sideways for months at a time, so how it is mounted and sealed decides how long it lasts.

The **indoor monitor** is a screen inside the house, usually in the hallway or the kitchen. It rings, shows you the camera, and lets you talk back. On most systems it also has the button that releases the door or gate.

Everything else — app answering, recording, multiple monitors — is a variation on those two.

## Wired or Wi-Fi

This is the decision that shapes the installation, and it is mostly decided by your building rather than your preference.

**Wired systems** run a cable from the outdoor unit to the monitor. The picture and the call travel down that cable, so they do not care whether your Wi-Fi reaches the gate. That steadiness is the reason to choose wired, and it is the better option whenever the cable can be run without tearing up a finished wall — a house under construction, a renovation, or a run that can follow existing conduit.

**Wi-Fi and IP systems** skip the cable. The outdoor unit joins your network and the call travels over it. In a house that is already finished, this is usually the deciding advantage: no chasing walls, no conduit along a compound wall.

The catch is the one you would expect. Your Wi-Fi has to actually reach the gate, properly, not just show one bar on a phone held at the right angle. A gate thirty metres from the router with a concrete wall in between is a weak Wi-Fi location, and a door phone that drops its call half the time is worse than no door phone at all. Where coverage is marginal, the honest answer is either to extend the network properly or to run the cable — and since we also do [networking and office WiFi](/networking), we would rather tell you that at the survey than leave you to discover it.

## Answering on your phone

On selected models, the call also reaches a mobile app. The outdoor unit rings, and your phone rings with it, wherever you are.

This is the feature people most often assume is standard and most often is not. It is worth being specific about what you want from it, because there are two quite different versions:

- **Answering while you are at home**, from whichever room you happen to be in rather than walking to the monitor.
- **Answering while you are away**, which is the one that matters if the house is sometimes empty, or if you are abroad and a caretaker handles the property.

Both exist on selected models. Visitor snapshots and recording — a log of who called while you were out — are also available on selected models. None of these can be added later to a unit that does not support them, so they belong in the conversation before the model is chosen.

## Power cuts

The units run on mains power. Left alone, they stop when the power does.

Where that matters, we wire the system to your home inverter connection during installation, which is the same approach we take with AC gate motors. It is a small thing to plan for at installation and an awkward one to retrofit, so it is worth saying at the survey if your supply is unreliable. Some models hold a short internal backup of their own, but how long varies by model and it is not something to build a plan around.

## Opening the gate from the indoor unit

On compatible setups, the indoor monitor — or the app, on models that have one — can trigger an [automatic gate](/entrance-automation/automatic-gates) or release a [smart door lock](/entrance-automation/smart-door-locks). You see the visitor, you speak to them, and you let them in without leaving the room.

This is the point where a door phone stops being a doorbell with a camera and starts being part of an entrance. It depends on both devices supporting it, which is why we check the specific combination before quoting rather than assuming it will work. When it does work, it is the single feature people say they use most.

## Apartments with several flats

A block is a different job. One outdoor panel at the main entrance calls the specific flat, and each flat has its own monitor.

What makes this harder than a house is not the electronics but the building. The panel has to be sized for the number of flats, and the cabling has to reach every one of them. In a building under construction that is straightforward. In a finished building it depends entirely on what risers and conduit already exist, and that is the thing to establish before anyone quotes a figure. We cover apartments across Kottayam, Ernakulam, Pathanamthitta and Alappuzha, and for these the survey is not a formality.

## What we check at the survey

In roughly this order:

1. **The distance** from the outdoor unit to where the monitor will go, and whether a cable can follow that route.
2. **Your Wi-Fi at the gate**, if wired is impractical — measured, not assumed.
3. **Where power comes from**, and whether the inverter connection should be brought into it.
4. **The mounting position**, which decides what the camera sees. Too high and you get the tops of heads; too low and it is easy to obscure.
5. **What it needs to talk to** — an existing gate motor, a lock, or cameras — and whether those will actually integrate.
6. **How many monitors**, and where, which is usually one more than people first think.

From that we give a fixed written quote naming the exact units and saying plainly which features that model supports. Nothing is ordered before you approve it.
